@ Numbers, relationships and growth
units|Units and basis points|units|A rate is a proportion, not a cash amount. Before multiplying anything, ask what each number measures. One percent means one part in a hundred; one basis point means one part in ten thousand.|Multiply a notional by a decimal rate change to obtain a cash change in a simple linear example. Converting units first prevents an error of a hundred times. This example is interest arithmetic, not a bond DV01 calculation.|cash = notional × basis points / 10,000|On 100 currency units, 25 basis points corresponds to 0.25 units.|Double the notional and predict the cash change before moving the control.|Why is 25 basis points not 25 percent?|A basis point is 0.01 percentage points. Therefore 25 basis points is 0.25 percent.|Rates, spreads and sensitivity reports require consistent units.
fractions|Fractions, weights and averages|weights|Imagine splitting a pot between two investments. A weight tells you what fraction of the pot belongs to each. A weighted average gives a larger voice to the larger allocation.|For a fully invested portfolio, the weights sum to one. Multiply each return by its weight, then add. Short positions can make weights negative, but this first experiment uses allocations between zero and one.|portfolio return = w × r₁ + (1 − w) × r₂|With 25% in an asset returning 8% and 75% returning 4%, the portfolio earns 5%.|Move the weight from zero to one and follow the straight line.|Can a long-only weighted average exceed both inputs?|No. Nonnegative weights summing to one place the result between the inputs.|Portfolio aggregation begins with weighted sums.
signed|Signed quantities and netting|signed|A positive holding and a negative holding point in opposite directions. Selling an asset short creates a negative position; an upward price move then produces a loss instead of a gain.|Add signed exposures only when they refer to the same measured quantity and compatible units. A zero net exposure can hide substantial gross positions and does not cancel every kind of risk.|net = long − short; gross = long + short|A long exposure of 7 and short exposure of 5 give net 2 and gross 12.|Match the two positions. Inspect what remains in the gross total.|Does zero net exposure mean there is no liquidity risk?|No. Separate positions can still require funding, collateral or settlement even when a selected net sensitivity is zero.|Hedging uses direction as well as magnitude.
algebra|Equations as balance|linear|An equation says two descriptions have the same value. Solving it means finding an input that keeps this balance true. Think of removing equal amounts from both sides of a scale.|For a straight-line rule, first undo the fixed addition, then undo the multiplication. Division is allowed only by a nonzero coefficient. The visual shows both the rule and the target you are trying to reach.|a x + b = 10 ⇒ x = (10 − b) / a|If 2x + 4 = 10, subtract 4 to get 2x = 6, then divide by 2 to get x = 3.|Change the slope while keeping the target fixed.|What happens when the slope becomes very small?|A small change in the target requires a large change in the solution. Inverting a nearly flat relationship is unstable.|Calibration is often an equation solved for an unknown parameter.
functions|Functions, domains and composition|function|A function is a rule that assigns one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of inputs for which the rule makes sense. Drawing the graph reveals behaviour that a formula can hide.|The experiment transforms a parabola by shifting it and changing its height scale. To compose functions, feed the output of the first rule into the second. The order matters: shifting before squaring differs from shifting after squaring.|f(x) = a(x − b)²|For a = 2 and b = 1, the input x = 3 gives 2(3 − 1)² = 8.|Move the horizontal shift and locate the new minimum.|Is squaring reversible without restricting the domain?|No. Both positive and negative inputs can have the same square. Restricting to nonnegative inputs makes the square-root inverse single-valued.|Prices are functions of market inputs, with domains imposed by the model.
exponents|Repeated growth and compounding|compound|Simple interest adds the same amount each period. Compound interest also earns interest on earlier interest. Repeated multiplication, rather than repeated addition, creates the curved growth path.|With n compounding periods per year, divide the annual nominal rate by n and apply that smaller growth factor n times per year. Letting n increase leads to continuous compounding for a fixed nominal rate.|V(t) = V₀(1 + r/n)^(nt); continuous limit = V₀e^(rt)|100 compounded annually at 10% becomes 121 after two years; simple interest gives 120.|Increase the compounding frequency and compare with the continuous limit.|Why does the gap grow with time?|Interest earned earlier has more opportunities to earn further interest.|Discounting reverses compounding to value future cash flows today.
logs|Logarithms and additive returns|log|A logarithm answers a reverse growth question: what exponent produced this ratio? Natural logarithms turn multiplication into addition, making a chain of growth factors easier to describe.|A simple return is a proportional price change. A log return is the natural logarithm of the price ratio. Add log returns across time, then exponentiate to recover the cumulative growth factor. Positive prices are required.|log return = ln(P₁/P₀); ln(ab) = ln(a) + ln(b)|A 10% rise followed by a 10% fall gives 1.1 × 0.9 = 0.99, a 1% loss.|Compare the straight-line approximation r with ln(1 + r).|Why do equal percentage gains and losses fail to cancel?|The loss is applied to a different base. Log returns capture the multiplicative combination exactly.|Log returns are central to compounding and positive-price models.
sequences|Sequences, series and perpetuities|series|A sequence is an ordered list; a series adds its terms. Repeatedly taking a fraction of what came before creates a geometric sequence. The sum can approach a finite ceiling even though infinitely many positive terms are added.|Multiply the partial sum by the common ratio and subtract. Most terms cancel, leaving a compact formula. A geometric infinite sum converges only when the absolute ratio is below one.|Σ from k=1 to n of q^k = q(1 − q^n)/(1 − q)|For q = 1/2, the first three terms sum to 0.875 and the infinite sum is 1.|Increase the number of terms, then move the ratio towards one.|Why does convergence become slower near one?|Each new term shrinks only slightly, so the remaining tail stays large for longer.|An equally spaced perpetuity discounts payments into a geometric series.
@ Calculus: change and accumulation
limits|Limits and approaching a value|limit|A limit describes what outputs approach as inputs move closer to a point. The function need not be defined at the point itself. Watching nearby values prevents confusing an approaching value with a value actually attained.|Factor x² − 1 into (x − 1)(x + 1). Away from x = 1 the quotient becomes x + 1, even though the original expression is undefined at one. A limit captures the nearby behaviour.|lim as h→0 of ((1+h)² − 1)/h = 2|At h = 0.1 the quotient is 2.1; at h = 0.01 it is 2.01.|Shrink the step and follow the approach without dividing by zero.|Must a limit equal the function value at the point?|Only if the function is continuous there. A missing or redefined point can have the same surrounding limit.|Limits support derivatives, continuous compounding and convergence arguments.
continuity|Continuity and payoff kinks|kink|A continuous graph can be followed without jumping, but it can still have a sharp corner. A call payoff joins a flat region to a rising line at the strike. The value is continuous there while the slope changes abruptly.|Continuity requires the function value and the approaching values to agree. Differentiability is stronger: slopes from both sides must agree as well. The payoff makes this distinction visible without complicated notation.|payoff(S) = max(S − K, 0)|At strike 100, prices 99, 100 and 101 produce payoffs 0, 0 and 1.|Move the strike and compare the left and right slopes.|Does a continuous payoff have a derivative everywhere?|No. At the strike, a call payoff has left slope zero and right slope one.|Kinks explain why some numerical Greeks become delicate near expiry.
derivative|Derivatives from a shrinking interval|derivative|Average speed divides distance by elapsed time. A derivative applies the same idea to a function, then shrinks the interval until it describes a local rate of change. A secant line becomes a tangent.|For x², expand the numerator before cancelling h. The difference quotient is 2x + h, so its limit is 2x. The derivative is a local statement, not an exact prediction for any finite move.|f′(x) = lim as h→0 [f(x+h) − f(x)]/h|At x = 2, a step of 0.1 gives slope 4.1; the tangent slope is 4.|Reduce the interval and compare the two slopes.|Why does a tangent estimate worsen for larger moves?|It ignores the changing slope over the interval.|Delta is a derivative of value with respect to an underlying price.
chain|The chain rule as linked changes|chain|Suppose one input changes an intermediate quantity, which then changes the output. A small movement travels through both relationships. The total local response multiplies the two response rates.|For f(x) = exp(ax), the inner rule ax changes at rate a. The outer exponential changes at its own current value. Multiplying gives a exp(ax). Units also multiply and cancel through the chain.|d f(g(x))/dx = f′(g(x))g′(x)|For exp(2x) at x = 0, the inner slope is 2 and outer slope is 1, so the total slope is 2.|Increase the inner scaling and watch the curve steepen.|Why is the outer derivative evaluated at the inner output?|That is the input the outer function actually receives.|Curve risk propagates through quotes, discount factors and instrument values.
product|Product and quotient rules|product|When both sides of a rectangle grow, the area changes through a strip on each side plus a tiny corner. Dividing by the step and taking a limit makes the corner contribution vanish.|This explains why the derivative of a product contains two terms. Differentiating a quotient can then be derived by treating the reciprocal as a power and applying the chain rule.|(uv)′ = u′v + uv′; (u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′)/v²|For u=x and v=x², the product is x³ and the derivative is x² + 2x² = 3x².|Compare the two contributions to the product derivative.|Why is multiplying the two derivatives wrong?|It keeps only the tiny corner effect and loses the first-order strips.|Discounted values combine a changing payoff estimate and a discount factor.
convexity|Curvature and Jensen’s inequality|convexity|A curved bowl sits below the straight chord connecting two points on it. Averaging the inputs and then applying the curve can therefore differ from averaging the outputs. The order of operations matters.|For a convex function, the output at a weighted average is no greater than the weighted average output. This holds for nonnegative weights summing to one. Concave functions reverse the inequality.|f(wx + (1−w)y) ≤ wf(x) + (1−w)f(y)|With x=0, y=2 and equal weights, the square of the average is 1 while the average square is 2.|Change the weight and observe the gap vanish at either endpoint.|Does uncertainty always increase the value of any payoff?|No. The direction depends on convexity and the distribution, with the relevant expectation finite.|Convex option payoffs help explain the value of dispersion.
taylor|Taylor expansions as local models|taylor|A tangent matches value and slope at one point. A quadratic approximation also matches curvature. Taylor expansion builds increasingly detailed local models from derivatives, rather than claiming a curve is globally polynomial.|Around zero, exp(x) begins with 1 + x + x²/2. The factorial in each denominator accounts for repeated differentiation. A remainder measures what was left out; small distance alone is not a universal error guarantee.|e^x = Σ x^k/k!; quadratic approximation = 1 + x + x²/2|At x=0.5, the quadratic gives 1.625 compared with e^0.5 ≈ 1.6487.|Increase polynomial order and compare errors near and far from zero.|Why should a risk approximation report the size of the shock?|A local approximation can be accurate for one shock size and poor for another.|Delta-gamma approximations are truncated Taylor expansions.
integral|Integration by adding thin slices|integral|To measure accumulated quantity, divide the interval into strips, estimate each strip’s contribution and add. Making the strips narrower leads to the definite integral when the function is integrable.|A rectangle’s height is the local function value and its width is the input interval. Their product carries the units of the accumulated quantity. Signed areas below zero subtract from the total.|∫ from 0 to 1 x² dx = 1/3|Four left rectangles give 0.21875; finer partitions approach 0.33333.|Increase the rectangle count and compare the estimate with the exact area.|Can a signed integral be zero while the graph is not zero?|Yes. Positive and negative contributions can cancel.|Continuous cash flows and probability expectations are accumulated by integration.
ftc|Why integration reverses differentiation|ftc|Let an area grow by moving its upper boundary. A tiny extension adds approximately the boundary height times the extension width. The rate of change of accumulated area is therefore the height of the original curve.|This is the fundamental theorem of calculus for a continuous integrand. If an antiderivative is known, subtracting its endpoint values computes the definite integral without explicitly summing infinitely many rectangles.|A(x)=∫₀ˣ t² dt=x³/3; A′(x)=x²|The area to x=2 is 8/3; its instantaneous growth rate there is 4.|Move the upper boundary and compare accumulated area with local height.|Why is an arbitrary integration constant irrelevant to a definite integral?|The same constant is subtracted at the two endpoints.|Cumulative probabilities and densities are linked by this principle where differentiable.
improper|Infinite intervals and tail integrals|improper|An infinite integration range does not automatically create an infinite answer. A decaying curve can add progressively less area. The question is whether the finite-cutoff integrals approach a finite limit.|For positive decay rate a, the exponential tail integrates to 1/a. A small rate spreads the same height over a longer region. Truncating at a finite horizon leaves a calculable omitted tail.|∫₀ᵀ e^(−at)dt=(1−e^(−aT))/a; tail=e^(−aT)/a|For a=1 and T=3, accumulated area is about 0.9502 and the remaining tail is 0.0498.|Extend the cutoff and inspect the tail rather than only the visible curve.|Why is a small curve height not enough to dismiss the tail?|A small height can persist over a very long interval.|Long-horizon discounting and tail expectations require convergence checks.
@ Linear algebra: geometry of many risks
vectors|Vectors and linear combinations|vectors|A vector is an ordered collection of quantities. In a picture it can be drawn as an arrow; in a portfolio it can list holdings or exposures. Adding vectors combines each component separately.|A linear combination scales vectors and then adds them. Using two nonparallel directions can reach every point in a plane. Negative coefficients reverse an arrow instead of creating a new rule.|v = a(1,0) + b(0,1)|With a=2 and b=3, the resulting vector is (2,3), whose length is √13.|Change one coordinate at a time and then both together.|Why must we distinguish vector length from the sum of coordinates?|Opposing signed coordinates can cancel in a sum even while the vector has substantial magnitude.|Portfolios and risk-factor exposures are naturally represented as vectors.
dot|Dot products and projections|dot|The dot product measures alignment. Two long arrows pointing at right angles have zero dot product, while parallel arrows reinforce one another. Projection keeps the part of one arrow that lies along another.|Multiply matching coordinates and add. Dividing by a unit direction’s length is unnecessary because it is already one. For a non-unit direction, divide appropriately to recover the projected vector.|u·v = Σuᵢvᵢ = ‖u‖‖v‖cos θ|The dot product of (2,1) and (1,3) is 5.|Rotate one arrow and find the perpendicular position.|Can a zero dot product mean both vectors are nonzero?|Yes. Orthogonal vectors have no component along each other.|Portfolio value is a dot product of holdings and instrument prices.
matrix|Matrices transform space|matrix|A matrix tells you where the coordinate directions move. Once those destinations are known, every other point follows by the same linear-combination rule. A square grid makes the transformation visible.|The first column is the transformed first basis vector; the second column is the transformed second basis vector. A shear slants the grid without needing a curved mapping. The determinant measures signed area scaling.|A(x,y) = (x + a y, b y); det(A)=b|For a=1 and b=2, (1,1) maps to (2,2), and areas double.|Change shear and vertical scale separately.|Why does shear alone preserve area in this example?|It changes the slant but not the base-height product; the determinant remains one.|Matrices map factor changes into many instrument responses at once.
systems|Solving simultaneous equations|systems|Two equations describe two lines. Their intersection gives a pair of values satisfying both relationships at once. If the lines become nearly parallel, the solution becomes sensitive to tiny changes.|Elimination removes one unknown by subtracting equations. Matrix methods generalise this process to many unknowns. A unique solution requires independent equations; more equations alone do not guarantee more information.|x+y=2; x+a y=b|For a=2 and b=3, subtraction gives y=1 and then x=1.|Move the second slope close to the first and watch the intersection.|Why is a nearly singular system dangerous in calibration?|Small data errors can cause large changes in the inferred parameters.|Replication and calibration often solve linear systems.
rank|Rank, span and redundancy|rank|Two arrows may look like two pieces of information but still point along the same line. Their span is the set of combinations they can create. Rank counts the number of independent directions, not the number of columns.|When one column is a multiple of another, a matrix cannot reach every output direction. Its null space contains input combinations mapped to zero. These combinations describe redundancy in the representation.|A=[[1,2],[a,b]]; det(A)=b−2a|If a=1 and b=2, the second column is twice the first and the rank is one.|Set the determinant to zero, then move slightly away.|Does a tiny nonzero determinant make the system numerically reliable?|No. Formal invertibility can coexist with severe sensitivity to errors.|Redundant risk factors can make regression and hedge construction unstable.
eigen|Eigenvectors as unchanged directions|eigen|Most arrows rotate when a matrix transforms them. An eigenvector is special: it stays on the same line and only changes length or direction. Its eigenvalue is the scaling factor.|For a symmetric two-factor matrix with equal diagonal entries, the common and opposing directions are eigenvectors. Positive and negative correlation change which direction stretches more.|A=[[1,ρ],[ρ,1]] has eigenvalues 1+ρ and 1−ρ|At ρ=0.8, the common direction scales by 1.8 and the opposing direction by 0.2.|Move correlation through zero and see the dominant direction change.|Why is an eigenvector’s sign arbitrary?|Multiplying it by minus one represents the same line and satisfies the same eigenvalue relation.|Eigenvectors describe dominant modes of joint market movement.
psd|Covariance matrices and nonnegative risk|psd|A variance cannot be negative. A valid covariance matrix must therefore produce nonnegative variance for every portfolio direction, not merely for a few chosen weights.|This property is positive semidefiniteness. For two standardised factors, correlation must lie between minus one and one. The quadratic form sums own-risk contributions and the interaction between the factors.|Var(w₁X+w₂Y)=w₁²+w₂²+2ρw₁w₂|Equal weights with ρ=0 give variance 0.5; with ρ=1 they give variance 1.|Vary correlation and inspect the variance of every allocation.|Can individually plausible pairwise correlations form an invalid larger matrix?|Yes. In higher dimensions, joint positive semidefiniteness imposes additional constraints.|Portfolio risk and capital aggregation depend on valid covariance structures.
cholesky|Cholesky factors and correlated shocks|cholesky|Start with two independent standardised shocks. Keep the first and mix part of it into the second. The shared piece creates correlation, while an independent remainder preserves total variance.|For correlation ρ, combine ρZ₁ with √(1−ρ²)Z₂. The squared coefficients add to one. Cholesky factorisation extends this construction to positive-definite covariance matrices; singular cases need care.|X=Z₁; Y=ρZ₁+√(1−ρ²)Z₂|At ρ=0.6, the coefficients are 0.6 and 0.8, whose squares sum to one.|Move correlation from negative to positive and inspect the cloud.|Why is the independent coefficient not simply 1−ρ?|Variance depends on squared coefficients, not their ordinary sum.|Simulation generates correlated market shocks using matrix factors.
pca|Principal components and explained variance|pca|A cloud may stretch mainly along one direction. Projecting onto that direction preserves more variation than projecting onto a short direction. Principal component analysis orders orthogonal directions by captured variance.|For a covariance matrix, eigenvectors provide the component directions and eigenvalues their variances. Centring is essential; scaling changes the question being answered. Explained variance measures reconstruction of variation, not predictive usefulness.|explained fraction of component i = λᵢ / Σλⱼ|For eigenvalues 1.8 and 0.2, the first component explains 90% of total variance.|Increase the magnitude of correlation and watch one mode dominate.|Does high explained variance prove a factor predicts returns?|No. PCA summarises variation; forecasting requires a separate model and evaluation.|Yield-curve risk is often described using dominant movement patterns.
svd|Singular values and conditioning|svd|A general linear map can be understood as a rotation, a stretch along perpendicular axes and another rotation. Singular values are the stretch factors. A tiny stretch loses information when measurement errors are present.|Inverting that tiny stretch magnifies errors. The condition number compares largest and smallest singular values. Truncation or regularisation can reduce instability, but it changes the problem and introduces bias.|A=UΣVᵀ; condition number=σmax/σmin|For diagonal stretches 2 and 0.1, the condition number is 20.|Shrink the smaller stretch and inspect how an input error is amplified.|Why is an exact algebraic inverse not always a good numerical method?|The data and arithmetic are imperfect, and inversion can magnify both errors.|Conditioning matters in regressions, calibration and curve construction.
@ Many inputs and optimisation
partial|Partial derivatives and slices|partial|A surface depends on more than one input. To isolate one influence, freeze the other inputs and walk along a slice. The slope on that slice is a partial derivative.|For f(x,y)=x²+2y², moving in x while holding y fixed gives slope 2x. Holding an input fixed is part of the question; allowing it to move produces a different total response.|∂f/∂x=2x; ∂f/∂y=4y|At (1,2), the two partial derivatives are 2 and 8.|Move the selected point and compare slopes in the two directions.|Why can a reported sensitivity depend on what was held constant?|Different constraints define different paths through the same surface.|Greeks isolate price responses to individual model inputs.
gradient|Gradients and the steepest direction|gradient|Collect all partial derivatives into an arrow. Its direction points towards the fastest local increase when distance is measured in the usual Euclidean way. Moving against it gives the steepest local decrease.|A directional derivative is the dot product of the gradient and a unit direction. Rescaling coordinates changes the geometry, so steepest descent depends on parameter units and scaling.|∇f=(∂f/∂x,∂f/∂y); Dᵤf=∇f·u|For x²+2y² at (1,1), the gradient is (2,4).|Compare a move along a contour with a move across contours.|Why is the gradient perpendicular to a smooth level curve?|Along a level curve the value does not change, so the directional derivative is zero.|Optimisers use gradients to adjust model parameters efficiently.
hessian|Hessians and cross-effects|hessian|Local slopes themselves change as you move. The Hessian collects these second derivatives. Its off-diagonal entries show how changing one input affects the sensitivity to another.|For a smooth function, mixed partials agree under standard continuity conditions. A positive-definite Hessian makes the local quadratic model a bowl; a negative eigenvalue reveals a direction of downward curvature.|f=x²+y²+ρxy; H=[[2,ρ],[ρ,2]]|At ρ=1, the Hessian eigenvalues are 3 and 1, so the quadratic is a strict bowl.|Change the cross-effect and observe rotated contours.|Why are separate one-input gamma measures not the whole story?|Cross derivatives contribute when inputs move together.|Multifactor second-order risk includes cross-gamma terms.
jacobian|Jacobians and changing coordinates|jacobian|Changing coordinates can stretch area as well as reposition points. The Jacobian is a matrix of local partial derivatives that describes the small-scale linear transformation.|Its determinant gives the local signed area factor in two dimensions. Density transforms use the absolute factor because probability mass cannot become negative. The mapping must meet the relevant invertibility conditions.|For u=ax, v=by: abs(det J)=abs(ab)|If both axes double, area becomes four times as large and a uniform density becomes one quarter as high.|Change the two stretches and compare area with density.|Why must density decrease when the same probability fills more area?|Probability is density times area, and the total mass is preserved.|Distribution transformations and numerical integration depend on Jacobians.
double|Double integrals and joint mass|double|An area integral stacks tiny columns over a plane. Each column contributes height times its small base area. For a joint density, adding columns over a region gives a probability.|A valid density is nonnegative and integrates to one. Integrating out one coordinate produces a marginal density. For independent uniforms on the unit square, rectangle probabilities equal their areas.|P(X≤a,Y≤b)=∫₀ᵃ∫₀ᵇ 1 dy dx=ab|Independent uniform variables satisfy P(X≤0.4,Y≤0.5)=0.2.|Resize the highlighted rectangle and compare side lengths with joint mass.|Would the same area rule hold for every joint distribution?|No. A nonuniform density weights different regions differently.|Joint integration combines multiple uncertain risk drivers.
descent|Gradient descent and step size|descent|To find the bottom of a bowl, repeatedly move downhill. A short step makes slow progress; a very long step can jump across the bottom and move farther away. The slope gives direction, but step size controls stability.|For f(x)=x²/2, subtracting η times the gradient gives xnext=(1−η)x. The iterates converge to zero when 0<η<2; at the boundary they can oscillate without shrinking.|xₖ₊₁=xₖ−ηf′(xₖ)|With x₀=4 and η=0.5, successive values are 2, 1 and 0.5.|Move the step size above one, then above two.|Does a decreasing local slope guarantee every step improves the objective?|No. A large step can overshoot the region described by the local approximation.|Calibration requires stable optimisation, not just derivative evaluation.
lagrange|Constraints and Lagrange multipliers|lagrange|An unconstrained best choice may be impossible because weights must sum to one or an exposure must match a target. A constraint restricts movement to a curve or surface.|At a smooth constrained optimum, the objective gradient has no component along an allowed direction. It aligns with the constraint gradient. The multiplier quantifies local sensitivity to the constraint level when regularity holds.|minimise x²+y² subject to x+y=c ⇒ x=y=c/2|For c=2, the closest point on the line to the origin is (1,1), with objective 2.|Move the required total and watch the tangency point.|Why is setting both objective partial derivatives to zero wrong here?|That gives the unconstrained optimum, which may violate the constraint.|Portfolio construction often combines an objective with exposure constraints.
efficient|Convex portfolios and efficient choices|efficient|Two assets can combine to reduce volatility when their returns are imperfectly correlated. Plot every allocation by its risk and expected return to see feasible trade-offs instead of guessing a best weight.|Variance is a quadratic function of weights; expected return is linear. With a positive semidefinite covariance matrix, variance is convex. An efficient choice cannot improve return without increasing risk among the allowed portfolios.|σp²=w²σ₁²+(1−w)²σ₂²+2w(1−w)ρσ₁σ₂|For two 20% volatility assets with zero correlation, equal weights give about 14.14% volatility.|Change correlation and inspect the diversification benefit.|Is the lowest-volatility allocation always the best investment?|No. The objective, expected returns, constraints and estimation uncertainty also matter.|Mean-variance geometry clarifies portfolio optimisation assumptions.
@ Probability: reasoning about uncertainty
sets|Outcomes, events and probability|sets|An outcome is one possible result; an event is a collection of outcomes. Probability assigns mass to events. Start with equally likely points so that probabilities can be counted before introducing densities.|The union means at least one event occurs. Adding their probabilities counts the overlap twice, so subtract it once. Mutually exclusive means no overlap; it does not mean independent.|P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)|On a fair die, A={1,2,3} and B={3,4} have union probability 4/6.|Resize two events and inspect the overlap.|Why are two nonempty mutually exclusive events not independent?|Observing one rules out the other, changing its probability to zero.|Default events and scenario sets need precise definitions.
counting|Counting and combinatorics|counting|To count sequences, multiply the number of choices at each stage. To count selections where order does not matter, remove the duplicate orderings. This distinction explains the binomial coefficient.|Choosing k items from n can be counted by ordered selection and then divided by k factorial. Factorials count arrangements of distinct items. The formula also works for choosing none or all.|C(n,k)=n!/[k!(n−k)!]|Choosing two names from five gives 10 pairs, not 20 ordered lists.|Move the selected count towards half the available count.|Why are middle-sized selections usually more numerous?|There are more ways to mix inclusion and exclusion than to choose almost everything or almost nothing.|Counting underlies discrete probability models and scenario trees.
conditional|Conditional probability as a smaller world|conditional|Once information is observed, outcomes inconsistent with it are removed. Conditional probability measures the remaining event mass relative to the reduced world, not relative to the original total.|The numerator is the overlap of the event and the information. The denominator is the information event’s probability, which must be positive. Conditioning can increase or decrease a probability.|P(A given B)=P(A∩B)/P(B)|If 20 of 100 borrowers are flagged and 5 flagged borrowers default, the observed default fraction among flagged borrowers is 25%.|Change the flagged share while keeping its default rate fixed.|Why should the denominator be the flagged group size?|That is the population remaining after the condition is known.|Risk estimates are conditional on information and portfolio selection.
bayes|Bayes’ rule and base rates|bayes|A warning signal can be common among bad outcomes and still mostly identify good ones if bad outcomes are rare. Draw the two populations first, then split each by whether the signal appears.|Bayes’ rule reverses a conditional relationship by accounting for prior prevalence. The evidence probability combines true and false signals. Sensitivity alone does not determine the probability of trouble after a signal.|P(D given F)=P(F given D)P(D)/[P(F given D)P(D)+P(F given not D)P(not D)]|With 1% prevalence, 90% sensitivity and a 10% false-positive rate, the posterior is about 8.33%.|Keep signal quality fixed and increase prevalence.|Why can the same signal mean different things in different portfolios?|The base rate changes the relative numbers of true and false signals.|Credit screening and model diagnostics require base-rate awareness.
random|Random variables and distributions|discrete|A random variable assigns a number to each outcome. The distribution describes how much probability lands on each numerical value. It separates the outcome description from the number we choose to measure.|Different outcomes may map to the same value, so their probabilities add. A discrete distribution lists masses that must be nonnegative and sum to one. The experiment uses a two-outcome payoff.|P(X=−1)=1−p; P(X=2)=p|At p=0.4, a loss of 1 has probability 0.6 and a gain of 2 has probability 0.4.|Move the gain probability and watch probability mass transfer.|Can a random variable’s expected value be an impossible outcome?|Yes. The mean is a weighted balance point, not necessarily one of the allowed outcomes.|Financial payoffs are random variables defined on market outcomes.
expectation|Expectation as a balance point|expectation|Place probability mass at each payoff on a number line. The expectation is the balancing location. It summarises the centre but does not describe the spread or guarantee a typical realised result.|Multiply each value by its probability and add. Linearity of expectation holds even for dependent variables, provided the expectations exist. Independence is not required for adding expected values.|E[X]=Σpᵢxᵢ|For payoffs −1 and 2 with gain probability 0.4, the expectation is 0.2.|Find the probability where the balance point reaches zero.|Does a positive expectation rule out frequent losses?|No. Frequency and magnitude both matter, and many losing outcomes can be offset by a few large gains.|Expected losses and expected payoffs begin with weighted outcomes.
variance|Variance, standard deviation and scale|variance|Two distributions can balance at the same point but spread out very differently. Squaring deviations prevents positive and negative differences from cancelling. Their average is the variance.|Standard deviation takes the square root to restore the original units. More reliable information receives more weight.|In the scalar identity-observation case, gain equals predicted variance divided by predicted plus measurement variance. The update shifts the predicted mean towards the observation. Exact Gaussian interpretation requires a correctly specified linear Gaussian model.|K=P/(P+R); updated mean=m+K(y−m); updated variance=(1−K)P|If P=R=1, predicted mean is 0 and observation is 2, the updated mean is 1 and variance is 0.5.|Increase measurement noise and inspect how the posterior trusts the prediction more.|Why is the gain determined by uncertainty rather than the observation’s magnitude alone?|The update balances relative reliability, while the innovation measures disagreement.|State-space models infer latent economic factors or evolving parameters from noisy measurements.
kkt|Inequality constraints and active boundaries|kkt|An optimum can occur at a boundary even when the objective slope is not zero. At that point, the direction that would improve the objective may be forbidden. Active constraints describe the boundaries that are binding.|Minimise (x−a)² subject to x≥0. If a is positive the unconstrained optimum is feasible; if a is negative the best allowed point is zero. Complementary slackness means a nonbinding inequality has a zero multiplier.|x*=max(a,0); with constraint −x≤0, multiplier λ=2max(−a,0)|For a=−2, the constrained solution is zero and multiplier is 4.|Move the unconstrained target across the boundary and watch the multiplier switch on.|Does a nonzero gradient at the constrained solution prove failure?|No. There may be no feasible improving direction. Under suitable convexity and constraint conditions, KKT conditions certify optimality.|Long-only weights, exposure caps and positivity constraints turn unconstrained fitting into constrained optimisation.
